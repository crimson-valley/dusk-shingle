# CHAPTER 001 — THE DRY PUMP

**Published:** 26 September 2026

**Reader navigation:** [INDEX](https://app.notion.com/p/INDEX-3e78742538ad81c5937ac1a54a9d77b7?pvs=21)

---

At 04:17, Pump Seven stopped.

Ilan Vey heard it before the alarm reached his terminal.

The east intake house had its own language. Motors had a pitch when they were healthy, another when a bearing was beginning to complain, and a third when a machine had decided it would rather become expensive than continue working.

Seven had gone quiet.

Ilan was already out of his chair.

The control room lights made the windows black. Beyond them, the pumping district was still dark except for the maintenance road and the lamps along the canal. Dawn was an hour away.

The alarm arrived.

**P-07 — FLOW LOSS.**

A second line appeared.

**P-08 — LOW SUCTION PRESSURE.**

Ilan stared at the two readings.

“That's new.”

Sava Orr came through the doorway pulling on his jacket.

“Seven?”

“Seven and Eight.”

Sava looked at the screen. “Both?”

“Eight hasn't tripped. Yet.”

He pulled up the district schematic. Seven and Eight drew from adjacent wells and fed the same rising main. They were not supposed to fail together.

Sava picked up the radio.

“Crew to East Seven. Bring the vibration kit. And a pressure gauge.”

Ilan zoomed into the trend.

Seven's flow had fallen for twelve minutes before the shutdown. Not much at first. Then sharply.

The motor current had climbed.

That was familiar.

The suction pressure was not.

It had been falling for three days.

Ilan checked the previous shift's maintenance report. A note from 21:40:

**P-07 minor vibration. No abnormal temperature. Monitor.**

“Who inspected it?”

“Rao.”

“Bearing?”

“Cleared.”

Sava looked at him. “You think he missed something?”

“I think the machine is telling us something.”

“That is the sort of sentence people use before making mechanics miserable.”

Ilan grabbed his helmet.

“Come on.”

The pump house smelled of hot insulation and wet concrete.

The motor on Seven had stopped, but its housing was still warm. A thin stream of water ran beneath the isolation valve.

Rao stood beside the coupling with a flashlight.

“Nothing seized,” he said.

Ilan crouched beside the discharge line.

The pressure gauge read lower than the control room had reported.

“How long has this been leaking?”

Rao pointed to the floor.

“That's not from the pump.”

Water was coming through the expansion joint in the concrete trench.

Ilan followed the line of dampness with his light. It disappeared beneath the pump foundation.

“Shut Eight down.”

Sava looked at him.

“We need the flow.”

“If the pressure boundary is compromised, we need to know before we put another machine against it.”

Sava hesitated.

Then he gave the order.

Eight shut down.

The room became very quiet.

Ilan checked the well readings.

The water level had fallen another fourteen centimeters since midnight.

He refreshed the central dashboard.

There, the district aquifer level showed a decline of three centimeters.

Ilan refreshed it again.

Three.

He opened the raw well logger.

Fourteen.

“That's wrong.”

Sava looked over.

“What?”

“Central says three.”

“Central says a lot of things.”

Ilan pulled the last thirty days.

The raw logger showed a steady decline.

Not dramatic.

Steady.

The central graph had been smoothing the same series into something almost flat.

He opened the calibration record.

Last service: nineteen days ago.

Sensor drift estimate: acceptable.

He checked the second well.

Same direction.

Different magnitude.

The third.

Same.

His stomach tightened.

“Get me the manual depth reading.”

Rao stared at him.

“Now?”

“Now.”

Rao disappeared through the door.

Sava folded his arms.

“If this is a sensor problem, we have two dead machines for nothing.”

“If it's a sensor problem, we have a cheaper problem.”

“And if it isn't?”

Ilan looked through the open door toward the black basin beyond the pump house.

“Then we're late.”

At 04:52, the manual reading came back.

The water level was eleven centimeters below the central model's value.

Not fourteen.

Not three.

Eleven.

Ilan wrote it down.

Then he wrote the time.

04:52.

He did not tell Sava what he was thinking.

Not yet.

A single measurement could be wrong.

Two could be bad luck.

Three could be a pattern.

He needed more.

The radio crackled.

“Control, East Seven. We've got seepage under the foundation.”

“Volume?”

“Growing.”

Sava took the radio.

“Any structural movement?”

A pause.

“Can't tell.”

Sava looked at Ilan.

Ilan looked at the pump.

The repair crew could isolate the line, drain the affected section, replace the joint, and have Seven running before noon.

If the foundation was moving, the repair would be temporary.

If the water level was falling faster than the model showed, the district had a different problem.

He opened the maintenance allocation screen.

Seven's replacement coupling was in stock.

Eight's was not.

The district had one functioning spare pump.

Ilan closed the screen.

“Keep Seven isolated,” he said.

Sava's expression hardened.

“You're not repairing it?”

“Not until we know whether the foundation moved.”

“We have a production target.”

“I know.”

“We miss it, the Directorate gets involved.”

“I know.”

Sava leaned closer.

“Then decide what you're protecting.”

Ilan looked at the two dead pumps.

He thought of the pressure line under the street, the apartments beyond it, the clinic six blocks south, the factories that would start their first shift in three hours.

He thought of the fourteen centimeters that the central system had turned into three.

“I'm protecting the next failure.”

Sava studied him.

“That's not an answer.”

“No.”

Ilan picked up the radio.

“Rao. Get the old benchmark plates from the survey cabinet.”

Sava's head snapped toward him.

“Why?”

“I want to know if the floor moved.”

“You're a pump engineer.”

“Reliability.”

“Same bad habit.”

Ilan ignored him.

Rao's voice came over the radio.

“Which benchmarks?”

“The ones nobody uses.”

There was silence.

Then:

“Understood.”

At 05:16, the first trucks arrived on the maintenance road.

At 05:21, the Industrial Belt began drawing its morning allocation.

At 05:24, the district pressure dropped again.

Ilan watched the numbers change.

Seven remained isolated.

Eight remained isolated.

The raw well level continued downward.

The central dashboard remained calm.

He opened the city's water balance.

For the first time, he noticed a small line beneath the main graph.

**Recoverable storage: within seasonal tolerance.**

Ilan read it twice.

Then he opened the definition.

The system assumed that the basin would recover after pumping was reduced.

He looked through the window at the dark ground beyond the pump house.

The floor beneath his boots gave a faint metallic tick as the building cooled.

Or settled.

He could not tell which.

That was the problem.

He called Mara Sen.

The call rang six times.

She answered without greeting.

“Vey.”

“I have two pumps down in East Seven.”

“Mechanical?”

“Maybe.”

A pause.

“Maybe isn't useful.”

“I have three well readings that don't match the central model.”

Another pause.

“How far?”

“Eleven centimeters on the manual reading.”

Silence.

Then, very quietly:

“Send me the raw logs.”

“I will.”

“Do not send them to the district group.”

Ilan looked at Sava.

“Why?”

“I want to see the data before somebody explains it to me.”

The line went dead.

Ilan stood still.

Sava watched him.

“Well?”

Ilan looked again at the city dashboard.

The green line held steady.

Outside, the maintenance crew began digging around the foundation.

“Now we find out,” he said.

He sent the logs.

Then he turned off the central dashboard.

For the first time that morning, he trusted the ugly numbers.
